Glycolysis and lactate

4 boxes
Glycolysis

Pyruvate kinase deficiency

Enzyme affectedPyruvate kinase — last enzyme of glycolysis: PEP → pyruvate, making ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation.

  • The red cell lives on glycolysis → ATP production falls.
  • RBC damage → lysis → hemolytic anemia.
  • Liver unaffected — it still makes ATP through the ETC.
From the slides · Lecture 9, slide 38
Anaerobic exit

Lactic acidosis

Enzyme involvedLactate dehydrogenase — pyruvate + NADH → lactate + NAD⁺, the anaerobic exit that keeps glycolysis running.

  • Lactate above ~5 mM (normal 0.5–1) → most common metabolic acidosis, raised anion gap.
  • Driven by hypoxia → anaerobic metabolism: myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, shock.
  • Lactate level is used as a measure of those conditions.
From the slides · Lecture 9, slide 50
Anaerobic exit

Warburg effect (cancer)

Enzymes involvedHexokinase, PFK-1, pyruvate kinase — the rate-limiting glycolysis enzymes, over-expressed in tumors.

  • Glucose → lactate even with mitochondria present: 2 ATP instead of 36–38 → needs ~19× the glucose → huge consumption.
  • That appetite is what FDG-PET reads: ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose is taken up but can’t be metabolized.
From the slides · Lecture 9, slides 63–65
Anaerobic exit

Metabolic acidosis — where it comes from

OverviewNormal pH is 7.35–7.45; metabolic acidosis = pH below 7.35, from lactic acid or from ketoacids.

  • Lactic acid: chronic alcoholics, myocardial infarction, cancer, uncontrolled hemorrhage, PDH deficiency, Von Gierke disease.
  • Ketoacids: starvation, diabetes mellitus type I (more common) and type II.

PDH and the TCA cycle

3 boxes
PDH complex

PDHC deficiency

Enzyme affectedPDH complex — pyruvate → acetyl-CoA + NADH, the bridge from glycolysis into the TCA cycle.

  • Pyruvate shunted to lactate and alanine → congenital lactic acidosis (most common biochemical cause).
  • ATP falls — the NADH is lost and acetyl-CoA stops feeding the TCA cycle → neurological problems (the brain runs on TCA).
  • Severe lethargy, poor feeding, tachypnea — worse with illness, stress, high-carb intake. X-linked E1-alpha.
From the slides · Lecture 10, slides 14–15
PDH complex

Arsenic poisoning

Enzymes affectedPDH, α-ketoglutarate DH, branched-chain α-ketoacid DH — the lipoic-acid enzymes (PDH and α-KG DH are the two big NADH makers of pyruvate oxidation and the TCA cycle).

  • Pyruvate → lactate → lactic acidosis; ATP production drops.
  • Affects the brain: neurological disturbances and death.
From the slides · Lecture 10, slides 16, 26
PDH complex

PDHC deficiency — the clinical vignette (Farrah)

Enzyme affectedPDH complex — the CV case showing how the deficiency presents and is worked up.

  • 20-month-old: seizures after a respiratory infection; lethargy, hypotonia, poor feeding. Labs: lactate 7.2 mM, pyruvate raised, alanine raised, anion gap 21; lactate-to-pyruvate ratio < 20 points to PDHC deficiency.
  • Alanine is high because blocked pyruvate is shunted through ALT (and to lactate through LDH).
  • E1 mutations = 70% of cases; the E1 gene is on the X chromosome. Thiamine supplementation helps E1 mutations that affect TPP binding.
  • Management: confirm by enzyme assay / gene analysis; limit carbohydrate — ketogenic diet (ketones as alternative fuel); dichloroacetate stimulates residual PDHC; thiamine + lipoic acid; carnitine.
  • Differential for congenital lactic acidosis: pyruvate carboxylase deficiency, MELAS, biotin or thiamine deficiency, glycogen storage diseases.

Gluconeogenesis and alcohol

4 boxes
Gluconeogenesis

Pyruvate carboxylase deficiency

Enzyme affectedPyruvate carboxylase (biotin, mitochondrial) — pyruvate → oxaloacetate, the first step of gluconeogenesis.

  • Hypoglycemia — glucose can’t be built; corrects with IV glucose.
  • Backed-up pyruvate → lactic acidosis and hyperalaninemia.
  • Infancy: seizures, failure to thrive, developmental delays.
From the slides · Lecture 11, slides 21, 28–29
Gluconeogenesis

Von Gierke disease — GSD type Ia

Enzyme affectedGlucose 6-phosphatase (liver ER) — G6P → free glucose, the final release step of BOTH gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis.

  • Severe fasting hypoglycemia — lethargy, seizures, brain damage. Cannot fast: frequent meals, nocturnal glucose, cornstarch.
  • Trapped G6P → liver glycogen builds up (normal structure) → hepatomegaly; kidney enlargement too.
  • Glycolysis forced forward → lactic acidosis; lactate blocks renal uric acid excretion → hyperuricemia.
  • Acetyl-CoA pushed to triglycerides → hyperlipidemia; doll-like face.
  • Type Ib is the same picture from the G6P transporter (translocase) that carries G6P into the ER.
From the slides · Lecture 11, slides 43–46 · Lecture 12, slide 55
Ethanol

Alcohol (heavy drinking)

Enzymes involvedAlcohol DH + aldehyde DH — ethanol → acetaldehyde → acetate, dumping 2 NADH into the cytosol per ethanol.

  • NADH pushes pyruvate → lactate and OAA → malate → gluconeogenic precursors gone → hypoglycemia (worst fasting or on insulin; glycogen empties in hours).
  • Lactate rises → lactic acidosis; the acidity blocks uric acid excretion → gout.
  • Fat oxidation inhibited → fatty liver, TG → VLDL → hyperlipidemia; acetyl-CoA → ketone bodies → more acidosis.
From the slides · Lecture 11, slides 52–54
Ethanol

Methanol poisoning

Enzymes involvedAlcohol DH + aldehyde DH — the same two enzymes carry methanol → formaldehyde → formate.

  • Formaldehyde is toxic to the optic nerve → blindness.
  • High doses may be fatal.

Glycogen storage diseases

6 boxes · Ia is under gluconeogenesis
Glycogen synthesis

GSD type 0 — glycogen synthase deficiency

Enzyme affectedGlycogen synthase — adds glucose (α-1,4) from UDP-glucose to the growing chain; the rate-limiting step of glycogen synthesis.

  • No glycogen store to draw on between meals.
  • Liver form (GYS2): fasting hypoglycemia + ketosis from fat breakdown; improves with food.
  • Muscle form (GYS1): pain, weakness, syncope after activity; arrhythmia, risk of cardiac arrest and sudden death.
From the slides · Lecture 12, slides 31–32
Glycogen synthesis

GSD type IV — Andersen disease

Enzyme affectedBranching enzyme (glucosyl 4:6 transferase) — makes the α-1,6 branches that keep glycogen soluble and multiply its ends.

  • Glycogen with long outer branches → insoluble (amylopectinosis).
  • Failure to thrive; hepatosplenomegaly; progressive cirrhosis; muscle hypotonia.
  • Death typically by age five.
From the slides · Lecture 12, slide 33
Glycogenolysis

GSD type II — Pompe disease

Enzyme affectedLysosomal acid α-1,4-glucosidase (acid maltase) — digests the glycogen delivered to lysosomes by autophagy.

  • Glycogen accumulates in lysosomes — the only lysosomal glycogen storage disease.
  • Blood glucose normal — the cytosolic pathway is intact.
  • Liver, muscle and heart: muscle weakness, cardiomegaly (“Pompe affects the pump”).
From the slides · Lecture 12, slide 56
Glycogenolysis

GSD type III — Cori disease

Enzyme affectedDebranching enzyme — 4:4 transferase + α-1,6 glucosidase, clears the branch points glycogen phosphorylase can’t reach in glycogenolysis.

  • Glycogen with very short outer chains — only partly usable.
  • Fasting hypoglycemia, hepatomegaly, myopathy.
  • Treated with frequent high-carb meals + cornstarch; high protein works too (drives gluconeogenesis). ABCD: Andersen-Branching, Cori-Debranching.
From the slides · Lecture 12, slides 58–59
Glycogenolysis

GSD type V — McArdle disease

Enzyme affectedMuscle glycogen phosphorylase — cleaves α-1,4 bonds with Pi → glucose 1-phosphate; the rate-limiting step of glycogenolysis.

  • Muscle can’t tap its glycogen for energy → cramps on exercise; myoglobinemia and myoglobinuria.
  • No rise in blood lactate after exercise — no glycogen-fed glycolysis to make it.
  • Liver enzyme normal, so blood sugar is fine; benign, normal mental development.
From the slides · Lecture 12, slide 60
Glycogenolysis

GSD type VI — Hers disease

Enzyme affectedLiver glycogen phosphorylase — the same rate-limiting glycogenolysis step, in the tissue that guards blood sugar.

  • Liver glycogen can’t be broken down → extreme hepatomegaly.
  • Hypoglycemia milder than type Ia — the deficiency is usually only partial (complete would be fatal), and gluconeogenesis still works.

The other sugars

7 boxes
Fructose

Essential fructosuria

Enzyme affectedFructokinase (liver, kidney, intestine) — fructose → fructose 1-phosphate, the entry step of fructose metabolism.

  • Fructose can’t be metabolized → eliminated in urine (detected as a reducing sugar).
  • Benign — nothing toxic accumulates.
From the slides · Lecture 13, slide 9
Fructose

Hereditary fructose intolerance (HFI)

Enzyme affectedAldolase B — splits fructose 1-phosphate into DHAP + glyceraldehyde, the second step of fructose metabolism.

  • F1-P accumulates in the liver, trapping phosphate → Pi and ATP fall, AMP rises.
  • High AMP blocks gluconeogenesis → hypoglycemia; AMP → uric acid → hyperuricemia.
  • Appears when a baby starts food or formula; hepatic failure and death if fructose + sucrose aren’t removed from the diet.
From the slides · Lecture 13, slide 10
Fructose

High-fructose diet

Enzyme involvedFructokinase — fast, unregulated, and its products enter glycolysis BELOW PFK-1, past the only real brake.

  • ATP drained → AMP → uric acid → gout; uric acid blocks eNOS → less NO → hypertension.
  • Unbraked carbon → citrate → fat → VLDL → obesity and dyslipidemia.
  • F1-P → inactive (serine-phosphorylated) IRS-1 → insulin insensitivity → metabolic syndrome.
From the slides · Lecture 13, slides 12–15
Galactose

Lactose intolerance

Enzyme affectedLactase (β-galactosidase), jejunal brush border — splits lactose into galactose + glucose, the main dietary source of galactose.

  • Primary lactase deficiency: diarrhea, bloating, cramps; can increase with age.
  • Up to 75% of the world has some lactose intolerance — ~90% of adults of Asian or African descent, ~25% of Northern European descent.
  • Secondary: from intestinal injury — the first activity lost and the last to recover.
From the slides · Lecture 13, slides 29, 34
Galactose

Galactokinase deficiency

Enzyme affectedGalactokinase — galactose → galactose 1-phosphate, the entry step of galactose metabolism.

  • Galactosemia and galactosuria; no Gal 1-P accumulation in tissues.
  • Galactose shunted by aldose reductase → galactitol → cataracts.
  • Rare, autosomal recessive; much milder than classical galactosemia.
From the slides · Lecture 13, slide 32
Galactose

Classical galactosemia

Enzyme affectedGALT (uridyltransferase) — swaps galactose 1-P onto UDP, releasing glucose 1-P; the step that brings galactose into the glucose pool.

  • Galactose 1-P accumulates in tissues; galactose in blood and urine.
  • Mental retardation (slide wording), liver and kidney damage, cataracts.
  • Newborns drink up to 1 g galactose/day in milk → immediate treatment needed; on the NYS newborn screen.
From the slides · Lecture 13, slide 33
Polyol pathway

Diabetic sorbitol complications

Enzymes involvedAldose reductase makes sorbitol from glucose (spending NADPH); sorbitol dehydrogenase, which would clear it, is low or absent in lens, retina, nerve and kidney.

  • Hyperglycemia → sorbitol made and trapped → osmotic swelling of lens, retina and nerve cells.
  • Peripheral neuropathy, diabetic retinopathy, macular edema → poor vision.
  • High glucose also glycosylates proteins.

The pentose phosphate pathway and NADPH

8 boxes
Pentose phosphate

G6PD deficiency

Enzyme affectedGlucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase — the rate-limiting first step of the PPP, making NADPH; the red cell’s ONLY NADPH source.

  • Less NADPH → glutathione stays oxidized → H₂O₂ not cleared → ROS rise.
  • Membrane lipids and hemoglobin oxidized → Heinz bodies, lysis → hemolytic anemia, jaundice.
  • Triggered by infection (most common), fava beans, antimalarials, sulfonamides, mothballs.
  • X-linked; the most common disease-producing genetic abnormality.
From the slides · Lecture 14, slides 24–29
Pentose phosphate

G6PD deficiency — the clinical vignette (Mikolo)

Enzyme affectedG6PD — the CV case showing the trigger, the labs, and the work-up.

  • 10-month-old of Greek descent: jaundice + dark brown urine one day into an ear infection; maternal uncle had the same episodes when sick (X-linked).
  • Infection → oxidative stress → hemolysis: ↓ Hb and RBC (anemia), ↑ reticulocytes (marrow response), ↑ unconjugated bilirubin (heme breakdown → jaundice), hemoglobinuria (the dark urine).
  • Confirm: fluorescent spot test (screening), quantitative G6PD assay (not during acute hemolysis), peripheral smear, gene sequencing.
  • Avoid: fava beans, primaquine/chloroquine, sulfonamides, some antipyretics (not acetaminophen), mothballs. ~60% of episodes come from illness or infection alone.
  • Differential for hemolytic anemia: sickle cell, thalassemias, autoimmune, PK deficiency, malaria.

Pentose phosphate

Thiamine deficiency — Wernicke, beriberi

Enzymes affectedTransketolase (2-carbon transfers in the PPP — the blood assay for thiamine) plus PDH E1 and α-KG DH (energy production).

  • Beriberi → heart failure.
  • Wernicke encephalopathy: ataxia, ocular abnormalities, memory loss with confusion.
  • Often from alcoholism (poor nutrition).

Pentose phosphate

Sickle cell disease (contrast with G6PD)

Not an enzyme defectHemoglobin β chain — Glu → Val at position 6 gives HbS. On the slides as the comparison case for RBC instability.

  • Deoxygenated HbS clumps into tactoids → sickled RBC → vaso-occlusive crisis.
  • Anemia, severe pain, splenic sequestration, infections, strokes.
  • Prevented by avoiding hypoxia (heavy exercise, altitude, infection); hydroxyurea raises HbF.

Glutathione / ROS

ALS — SOD1 mutation

Enzyme affectedCu-Zn superoxide dismutase (SOD1), cytosolic — converts superoxide → H₂O₂ in the ROS defense chain.

  • Superoxide not cleared → progressive neurodegeneration: amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (Lou Gehrig’s disease).
  • Most cases carry a single point mutation (one amino acid).
From the slides · Lecture 15, slide 14
Glutathione / ROS

ROS damage — what fails when NADPH runs out

MechanismH₂O₂ + Fe²⁺ (Fenton reaction) → hydroxyl radical, the species that does the damage when the glutathione defense can’t clear the peroxide.

  • Lipids: membrane peroxidation → gradients collapse → cell death; oxidized LDL → cardiovascular disease.
  • Proteins: cross-linking and fragmentation; in the RBC, denatured Hb → Heinz bodies → hemolytic anemia.
  • DNA: 8-hydroxydeoxyguanosine, ~10,000 lesions per cell per day → mutations, carcinogenesis; aging.

Respiratory burst

Chronic granulomatous disease (CGD)

Enzyme affectedNADPH oxidase — spends NADPH to make superoxide inside the phagolysosome: the respiratory burst that kills bacteria.

  • No ROS in the phagolysosome → bacteria engulfed but not killed.
  • Granulomas — inflammation nodules holding sequestered, undestroyed bacteria.
  • Severe persistent infections.
From the slides · Lecture 15, slide 39
Cytochrome P450

P450 drug interactions

Enzymes involvedCytochrome P450 (CYP) monooxygenases — use NADPH to hydroxylate drugs (phase I) so they can be excreted; CYP1–3 handle 70–80% of drugs.

  • Induction → co-drug cleared faster → subtherapeutic: St John’s wort + cyclosporine (anti-rejection failure).
  • Inhibition → co-drug level climbs → toxic: grapefruit + statins → rhabdomyolysis.
  • Also activates prodrugs: cyclophosphamide → its DNA-crosslinking form.
