Companion to the Wall Chart · Lectures 9–15

Every disease,
from the missing protein to the bedside

Twenty-seven diseases, one paragraph each, written as a single causal chain: which protein is gone, what backs up behind it, what runs out in front of it, and what that produces in a patient. Each closes by naming the diseases it is confused with and the one finding that separates them.

How to read a paragraph

Four marks carry meaning and nothing else is colored. Red sans-serif is the protein that is missing or disabled — the lesion itself. Amber is something that rises or accumulates behind the block. Blue is something that falls or is lost in front of it. Teal is the finding that identifies the disease — the one line that, on its own, tells you which of the look-alikes you are holding.

The rail on the left of each entry carries the protein and the pathway, so the enzymes can be read straight down the page without breaking into the prose. Everything else is prose, because a mechanism is a sentence, not a row.

Each entry then closes twice. The chain, in one line compresses the whole mechanism into a single causal sentence — not every detail, only the links that push each other — because that compressed form is what you actually reach for under time pressure. Against the look-alikes names the diseases it is confused with and the finding that separates them.

Two rules that decide who gets sick

Almost every apparent inconsistency in this material resolves into one of two questions, and both are worth applying before reasoning about a pathway at all. The first is about tissue: an enzyme defect spares a tissue if that tissue expresses a different isoform, has mitochondria, or both. The red cell has neither, which is why it appears in every glycolytic disease on this list; skeletal muscle and brain usually have both, which is why they so often escape. Where they do not escape — triose phosphate isomerase, phosphoglycerate kinase — it is because those enzymes are single-gene with no tissue-specific isoform to fall back on.

The second is about labs: a metabolite change inside a cell only becomes a serum value if that cell type dominates the plasma pool for that metabolite. Red-cell pyruvate and lactate genuinely fall in pyruvate kinase deficiency, and serum lactate is still normal, because red cells contribute almost nothing to whole-body lactate. Muscle, by contrast, is the dominant lactate producer, which is why the flat lactate curve is measurable in the muscle glycogenoses and not in the red-cell ones. Reading a value as a whole-body balance rather than as a report from one cell is what keeps the two straight.

I

Digestion

1 disease
01
Lactose intolerance
Protein lost
lactase
Pathway
Brush-border digestion

Lactase sits at the tip of the intestinal villus, the most exposed position any brush-border disaccharidase occupies, which is why it is the first activity lost to mucosal injury and the last to come back. Without it lactose is never split into glucose and galactose, so an unabsorbable disaccharide stays in the lumen, pulls water in osmotically, and is then fermented by colonic bacteria into hydrogen, methane and short-chain fatty acids. The gas and the water together produce bloating, cramping, flatus and osmotic diarrhea an hour or two after milk, and nothing systemic at all, because the sugar never crosses the epithelium. Confirm it with a hydrogen breath test, a low stool pH and a high stool osmotic gap, and by symptoms resolving on withdrawal. The primary form is a genetically programmed decline in lactase after weaning and is the majority state worldwide — more than three quarters of the world's adults have some degree of it, around 90 percent of adults of Asian or African descent against roughly 25 percent of those of northern European descent, which is the population that kept the enzyme. The secondary form follows anything that injures the mucosa and recovers with it, and the categories are worth having as a list, because the same reasoning covers every disaccharidase: intestinal disease — colitis, gastroenteritis, celiac, and cystic fibrosis, in which mucosal lactase runs low; malnutrition, kwashiorkor above all, where there is not enough protein to build digestive enzymes at all; drugs such as amoxicillin-clavulanate or clindamycin, which destroy the colonic flora that would otherwise salvage what the small bowel failed to absorb; and the plainly acquired case, in which severe diarrhea strips the enzymes off the surface. The congenital form is rare and presents at the first feed.

The chain, in one lineNo lactase means lactose is never split, and only monosaccharides cross the epithelium — so the intact disaccharide stays in the lumen, holds water osmotically and is fermented by colonic bacteria into gas and short-chain fatty acids, giving diarrhea, bloating and an acidic stool with nothing systemic at all, because the sugar never got in.

Against the look-alikesCow's-milk protein allergy involves the same drink but is immune-mediated, so it does not scale with the sugar load and can occur on trivial exposure. Sucrase–isomaltase deficiency produces an identical picture triggered by sucrose and starch rather than milk. Glucose–galactose malabsorption, a defect in SGLT-1, causes diarrhea after any glucose or galactose in any form. And classical galactosemia shares the disaccharide but breaks it normally — the damage there happens after absorption, which is exactly why lactose intolerance never causes liver or brain disease.

II

Glycolysis

3 diseases
02
Pyruvate kinase deficiency
Protein lost
pyruvate kinase (PKLR, R isoform)
Pathway
Glycolysis, final step

Pyruvate kinase catalyzes the last step of glycolysis, PEP to pyruvate, one of the two steps that actually make ATP. The red cell has no mitochondria, so glycolysis is its only source of ATP, and losing the final step means ATP falls permanently. The Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase fails, the cell loses potassium and water, becomes dehydrated and rigid, takes on an echinocytic shape, and is picked out and destroyed in the spleen — a chronic extravascular hemolysis that needs no trigger and never remits. Because the block is at the very end of the pathway, everything upstream dams, and the one that matters is 1,3-BPG, which spills sideways into the Rapoport–Luebering shunt and drives 2,3-BPG up. That right-shifts the oxygen dissociation curve, so hemoglobin unloads oxygen more readily and the patient tolerates the anemia far better than the hemoglobin number predicts. Pyruvate and lactate inside the red cell fall, but serum lactate is normal, because red cells contribute almost nothing to whole-body lactate; serum LDH is high, but from hemolysis, not from glycolysis. Blood glucose is normal, and the reason is worth being exact about: PKLR has two promoters and makes the R isoform in the red cell and the L isoform in the hepatocyte, so the liver usually carries the identical defect — but it has mitochondria, it has a nucleus and can keep making fresh enzyme, and its actual job runs on glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, which bypass pyruvate kinase entirely at PEPCK. If anything the block pushes PEP upward into glucose synthesis. One assay trap follows from the same isoform logic: leukocytes and platelets express PKM2 from the other gene at normal activity, so a whole-blood assay that has not had its white cells removed reads falsely normal. Expect neonatal jaundice, chronic normocytic anemia with reticulocytosis, splenomegaly, pigment gallstones, a negative Coombs, and aplastic crisis with parvovirus B19.

The chain, in one lineNo pyruvate kinase leaves the red cell without its last ATP-generating step and with no mitochondria to make up the difference, so the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase fails, the cell dehydrates and stiffens and is culled in the spleen; meanwhile everything upstream dams, 1,3-BPG spills into the Rapoport–Luebering shunt, and the 2,3-BPG that results right-shifts the curve — so the anemia is chronic but unusually well tolerated, and no other tissue is involved because muscle and brain read a different gene.

Against the look-alikesG6PD deficiency is episodic and trigger-dependent with Heinz bodies and bite cells; pyruvate kinase deficiency is continuous and trigger-independent with no inclusions. Sickle cell is a structural instability of hemoglobin, not an energy failure. Hexokinase deficiency looks nearly identical clinically but the block sits upstream of 1,3-BPG, so 2,3-BPG is low, the curve left-shifts, and at the same hemoglobin the patient feels worse rather than better — the mirror image, and the cleanest illustration that where a block sits along a pathway decides how it feels. Skeletal muscle is untouched throughout, because muscle pyruvate kinase is transcribed from a different gene, PKM, which is why this disease is purely hematologic.

03
PFK-1 deficiency
(Tarui, GSD VII)
Protein lost
phosphofructokinase-1, M subunit
Pathway
Glycolysis, committed step

PFK-1 is the rate-limiting committed step, fructose 6-phosphate to fructose 1,6-bisphosphate, and it is a tetramer assembled from three different subunit genes — M, L and P. Mature skeletal muscle builds it from M alone, the red cell from M and L hybrids, the liver from L alone. So a mutation in the muscle subunit takes muscle activity to zero and leaves red-cell activity around half, and the two-tissue disease follows directly from that arithmetic. Muscle can now use neither circulating glucose nor its own glycogen anaerobically, so within the first minutes of hard exertion — exactly when glycolysis should be carrying the load — ATP fails and the patient gets cramps, myalgia, and with continued effort rhabdomyolysis and myoglobinuria. On ischemic forearm exercise testing there is no rise in venous lactate, with an exaggerated ammonia rise from the purine nucleotide cycle running in its place. The red cell, at half activity, gives a mild compensated hemolysis with reticulocytosis, hyperbilirubinemia and sometimes hyperuricemia. Because the block sits far upstream of 1,3-BPG, 2,3-BPG is low and the curve left-shifts. Blood glucose is normal: the liver's L₄ enzyme is intact, and more fundamentally hepatic glucose output never needed PFK-1 in the first place. Cognition is normal for the parallel reason — brain PFK-1 is built largely from the P and L subunits, so it keeps substantial activity even with M gone.

The chain, in one lineOne mutation in the M subunit takes muscle PFK-1 (pure M₄) to zero and red-cell PFK-1 (an M/L hybrid) to about half, so muscle cannot run glycolysis at pace and cramps within minutes of hard exertion with a lactate curve that never rises, while the red cell, at half activity and with no other ATP source, hemolyzes mildly — and because the block sits above 1,3-BPG, 2,3-BPG falls instead of rising.

Against the look-alikesMcArdle disease produces an almost identical muscle picture with the same flat lactate curve, and two things separate them. Give glucose: McArdle gets a second wind and improves, while Tarui gets worse, because a glucose load suppresses the free fatty acids and ketones that are the only fuel the muscle can still use. And check the blood count: Tarui has hemolysis, McArdle has none. Against pyruvate kinase deficiency, both are glycolytic enzymopathies but PK is red cell only while PFK-1 is red cell plus muscle, and their 2,3-BPG runs in opposite directions because the blocks lie on opposite sides of 1,3-BPG.

04
The Warburg effect
(Ras and cancer metabolism)
Protein lost
none — glycolysis driven, not blocked
Pathway
Aerobic glycolysis to lactate

The one entry here in which nothing is broken and a pathway is instead running far too hard. Tumor cells consume enormous quantities of glucose and convert it to lactate even in the presence of oxygen and intact mitochondria — which is what makes it aerobic glycolysis rather than the anaerobic kind, and what made it strange enough to be named. The arithmetic explains the appetite. Taking one glucose to lactate returns 2 ATP where taking it through the mitochondrion returns 36 to 38, so a cell working this way needs about nineteen molecules of glucose to match the yield of one, and its glucose consumption rises accordingly. It sustains that flux by raising expression and activity of exactly the three rate-limiting glycolytic enzymes — hexokinase, PFK-1 and pyruvate kinase — which is how an oncogenic growth signal, Ras among them, is translated into a metabolic phenotype: the mutated GTPase is locked in its active state, growth-factor signalling runs without a ligand, and the transcriptional program it drives includes the glucose transporters and those glycolytic enzymes. The reading of why has shifted. The old account — rapidly dividing cells need ATP fast — fits the numbers poorly, and the current one is that aerobic glycolysis appears in a heterogeneous group of tumor cells that are not dividing, and that routing carbon to lactate rather than through the electron transport chain reduces ROS production. On that reading the effect is a way tumor cells protect themselves from oxidative damage rather than a way to raise their energy yield. Clinically the appetite is the point: ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose is a glucose derivative taken up preferentially by tumor cells and then not metabolized, so it accumulates wherever uptake is highest — which is what an FDG-PET scan reads, both to find tumor and to follow its response to treatment.

The chain, in one lineAn oncogenic signal drives expression of the glucose transporters and of hexokinase, PFK-1 and pyruvate kinase, so the cell takes glucose to lactate with mitochondria fully available; at 2 ATP instead of 36 to 38 it must pull about nineteen times the glucose to break even, and that enormous uptake is simultaneously what lowers its ROS burden and what a PET scan sees.

Against the look-alikesEvery other raised lactate in this material comes from a block — hypoxia, PDH deficiency, arsenic, thiamine deficiency, the alcoholic cytosol — where pyruvate dams because it cannot go forward. Here nothing is blocked and oxygen is adequate; the lactate reports flux, not obstruction, which is why the patient has no acidosis to speak of and why correcting oxygenation changes nothing. Against ordinary anaerobic glycolysis, the distinction is the same one in miniature: lactate with oxygen present means glycolytic flux has outrun oxidation, not that oxidation was impossible.

III

Pyruvate → acetyl-CoA

2 diseases
05
PDH complex deficiency
Protein lost
PDH E1α · PDHA1 · X-linked
Pathway
Pyruvate into the TCA cycle

The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex is the irreversible door out of glycolysis and into the TCA cycle, and the commonest defect is in the X-linked E1α subunit. Pyruvate cannot become acetyl-CoA, so it dams and takes the only two exits left to it: lactate dehydrogenase converts it to lactate, and ALT transaminates it to alanine. The result is congenital lactic acidosis with hyperalaninemia. Because pyruvate rises alongside lactate — nothing downstream is consuming it — the lactate-to-pyruvate ratio does not climb, and the ratio is not elevated (your chart records this as a LOW ratio; the mechanistically exact statement is that cytosolic redox is untouched, so the ratio stays at or below its normal 10 to 20). Blood glucose is NORMAL, because gluconeogenesis is entirely intact. The brain is the tissue that fails, since it runs almost wholly on glucose oxidation, giving neonatal or infantile lactic acidosis with hypotonia, seizures, psychomotor delay, cerebral atrophy, agenesis of the corpus callosum and, in its classic form, Leigh syndrome. Neither oxygen nor IV dextrose helps, because the problem is neither delivery nor supply — dextrose simply feeds the block and drives the lactate higher. A ketogenic diet does help, because ketone bodies are metabolized straight to acetyl-CoA and enter past the closed door; thiamine and dichloroacetate are worth a trial. That treatment also explains why this disease hits the brain when the glycolytic enzymopathies above largely do not: glycolysis is a route with alternatives, while PDH is a gate with only one bypass. A neuron with a glycolytic block can still be fed lactate by neighboring astrocytes, and in starvation ketones cover much of its fuel — but lactate must become pyruvate and pass through PDH, so of every rescue route only the ketones enter beyond it.

The chain, in one lineThe irreversible door from glycolysis into the cycle is shut, so pyruvate backs up and leaves by its two free exits — LDH to lactate and ALT to alanine — giving congenital lactic acidosis with hyperalaninemia at a ratio that never climbs, because cytosolic redox is untouched; glucose stays normal because gluconeogenesis was never in the path, and the brain fails because every alternative fuel it has must still pass through this one gate.

Against the look-alikesPyruvate carboxylase deficiency shares the lactic acidosis and the hyperalaninemia, and is the mirror image in every other dimension: hypoglycemic rather than euglycemic, ratio high rather than not-high, corrected by IV glucose rather than worsened by it, provoked by fasting rather than by carbohydrate. Arsenic poisoning and thiamine deficiency reproduce this picture from outside the genome. Respiratory chain defects and MELAS also give congenital lactic acidosis, but with a high ratio, because their block sits downstream of pyruvate.

06
Arsenic poisoning
Protein lost
lipoic acid — PDH E2, α-KGDH, BCKDH
Pathway
Pyruvate into the TCA cycle

Arsenic attacks in two chemically separate ways. Arsenite binds the two neighboring sulfhydryl groups of lipoic acid and locks them, which disables every enzyme that carries lipoate — PDH E2, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and branched-chain ketoacid dehydrogenase. That closes the same door as PDH deficiency and then closes a second one a single turn into the TCA cycle, so pyruvate and lactate rise while oxidative ATP production collapses in exactly the tissues that depend on it. Arsenate does something entirely different: it is a phosphate mimic, and GAPDH will accept it in place of inorganic phosphate to form 1-arseno-3-phosphoglycerate, which then hydrolyzes spontaneously — so the substrate-level phosphorylation that should have happened at phosphoglycerate kinase never occurs and glycolysis's net yield drops from 2 ATP to zero while the pathway keeps running. Acutely the patient has a garlic odor on the breath with vomiting, rice-water diarrhea, hypotension, QT prolongation and encephalopathy on top of the lactic acidosis. Chronically: stocking-glove peripheral neuropathy, hyperkeratosis and hyperpigmentation of palms and soles, Mees' lines across the nails, and later skin, lung and bladder cancers. Treat with a dithiol chelator — dimercaprol or succimer — which offers the arsenic a pair of thiols it binds more avidly than lipoate.

The chain, in one lineArsenite locks the two thiols of lipoic acid, disabling PDH, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and the branched-chain complex together, so pyruvate dams and the cycle stalls one step in as well; arsenate does something separate, substituting for phosphate at GAPDH to give an ester that hydrolyzes spontaneously, so the phosphoglycerate kinase ATP is never collected and glycolysis nets zero while still running.

Against the look-alikesPDH deficiency gives the same laboratory picture from inside the genome, but arsenic is acquired, disables three complexes rather than one, and arrives with skin, nail and gastrointestinal findings that no inherited enzymopathy produces. Thiamine deficiency also takes out the same three complexes, but by removing a cofactor rather than poisoning one, and it additionally hits transketolase — which is why the assay for it is a pentose phosphate pathway enzyme.

IV

Gluconeogenesis

2 diseases
07
Pyruvate carboxylase deficiency
Protein lost
pyruvate carboxylase (biotin-dependent)
Pathway
Gluconeogenesis, first step

Pyruvate carboxylase is mitochondrial, biotin-dependent and switched on by acetyl-CoA, and it carboxylates pyruvate to oxaloacetate — simultaneously the first committed step of gluconeogenesis and the main anaplerotic refill of the TCA cycle. With no oxaloacetate, gluconeogenesis cannot start, so the infant becomes hypoglycemic during fasting and corrects immediately on IV glucose, since glucose supplied from outside is used perfectly normally. The failure is not only that glucose cannot be built — it is that acetyl-CoA cannot enter the TCA cycle without oxaloacetate to carry it. Citrate synthase needs both, oxaloacetate is the cycle's limiting substrate even in health, and the cycle constantly loses intermediates to biosynthesis; pyruvate carboxylase is the anaplerotic reaction that replaces them, so removing it drains the whole four-carbon pool. Acetyl-CoA cannot rebuild it, because PDH is irreversible and humans have no glyoxylate cycle — so fat, the one fuel a fasting infant has in abundance, supplies endless two-carbon units and not one molecule of the carrier they need. There is a cruelty in the detail that acetyl-CoA is the obligatory activator of pyruvate carboxylase: the signal is being generated at full volume and arriving at an enzyme that is not there. As acetyl-CoA and NADH accumulate they activate PDH kinase and switch PDH off from the inside, so of pyruvate's four exits the mutation removes one and closes a second. Pyruvate therefore leaves by the only two that cost nothing and have no gate — LDH to lactate and ALT to alanine — producing lactic acidosis with hyperalaninemia. The loss of oxaloacetate has a second consequence that is the diagnostic one: it breaks the malate–aspartate shuttle, so cytosolic NADH can no longer be handed into the mitochondrion, accumulates, and drives lactate dehydrogenase hard — giving a HIGH lactate-to-pyruvate ratio alongside a low β-hydroxybutyrate-to-acetoacetate ratio, the fingerprint of NADH stranded on the wrong side of the inner membrane while the matrix stays relatively oxidized. Shortage of aspartate also starves the urea cycle, so ammonia rises. It presents in infancy with seizures, failure to thrive and developmental delay. Biotin deficiency, though rare, impairs the same enzyme and reproduces part of the picture.

The chain, in one lineNo pyruvate carboxylase means no oxaloacetate, so gluconeogenesis cannot start and acetyl-CoA cannot enter the cycle; acetyl-CoA and NADH therefore accumulate, activate PDH kinase and shut PDH off from the inside, leaving pyruvate only the two free exits to lactate and alanine — and the same missing oxaloacetate breaks the malate–aspartate shuttle, stranding NADH in the cytosol and driving the ratio up.

Against the look-alikesPDH deficiency is the mirror: same lactic acidosis and hyperalaninemia, but euglycemic, ratio not raised, worsened by carbohydrate and helped by ketones — where this disease is worsened by fasting and helped by glucose. Von Gierke also gives hypoglycemia with lactic acidosis, but adds hyperuricemia, hyperlipidemia and a massive liver, and has no hyperammonemia. Fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase deficiency blocks the same pathway three steps later and produces fasting hypoglycemia with lactic acidosis too, but only after glycogen is exhausted, so it needs a longer fast to appear.

08
Von Gierke, GSD Ia
(and Ib)
Protein lost
glucose 6-phosphatase · Ib: G6P translocase
Pathway
Gluconeogenesis + glycogenolysis

Glucose 6-phosphatase sits in the ER membrane of liver, kidney and intestine and performs the final step that both glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis must pass through to release free glucose into blood. It is autosomal recessive, the commonest glycogen storage disease at about a quarter of all cases, and historically the first enzyme defect ever identified in a metabolic disorder. Losing it closes both routes at once, which is why this is the most severe hypoglycemia on the list: it appears within a few hours of a feed, does not respond to glucagon, and is non-ketotic. Meanwhile glucose 6-phosphate has nowhere to go and is forced down every side road available. Into glycolysis, giving pyruvate and then lactate — a lactic acidosis which incidentally serves as an alternative brain fuel, which is why these children tolerate glucose levels that would obtund anyone else. Into the pentose phosphate pathway, giving ribose 5-phosphate and PRPP, driving purine synthesis and turnover to hyperuricemia, compounded because the lactate competes with urate at the renal excretion transporter — hence gout. And into lipogenesis, giving hypertriglyceridemia, eruptive xanthomas and a fatty liver. At the bedside: a doll-like rounded face, a protuberant abdomen from massive hepatomegaly with renomegaly and no splenomegaly, growth failure, and glycogen of entirely normal structure, merely abundant. Later come hepatic adenomas with a risk of carcinoma, and renal disease. Type Ib loses the translocase that carries G6P into the ER rather than the phosphatase itself, is clinically indistinguishable, and adds neutropenia with recurrent infection and inflammatory bowel disease. Manage with continuous overnight feeds or uncooked cornstarch, and restrict fructose and galactose, since they too must exit through this enzyme.

The chain, in one lineGlucose 6-phosphatase is the single exit both glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis must pass through, so losing it closes both routes at once and glucose 6-phosphate has nowhere to go but sideways — down glycolysis to lactate, into the pentose phosphate pathway to PRPP and urate, and into lipogenesis to triglyceride — which is why one missing enzyme produces four abnormalities simultaneously.

Against the look-alikesThe milder liver glycogenoses — Cori III and Hers VI — leave gluconeogenesis intact, so their hypoglycemia is mild and KETOTIC with normal lactate and normal urate. That single comparison, ketones and lactate, separates von Gierke from all of them at the bedside. Against pyruvate carboxylase and fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase deficiency: those block gluconeogenesis alone, so glycogenolysis still works and glucagon still raises glucose for as long as the stores last. And against Cori again on structure — von Gierke's glycogen is normal, Cori's has short outer chains.

V

Glycogen synthesis and breakdown

6 diseases
09
GSD 0
Protein lost
glycogen synthase
Pathway
Glycogen synthesiz

The one entry on this list that is not a storage disease at all — there is too LITTLE glycogen, not too much. Glycogen synthase extends the α-1,4 chain, and without it the liver never builds a reservoir in the first place. That produces a characteristic swing across the day. After a meal, glucose arrives with nowhere to be stored, so it overflows into glycolysis and runs to lactate: postprandial hyperglycemia with lactic acidosis. A few hours later there is no glycogen to draw on and gluconeogenesis has not yet taken over, so the child has fasting hypoglycemia — and because fatty acid oxidation is entirely intact, it is a KETOTIC hypoglycemia. The liver is normal in size. The muscle form, from the separate GYS1 gene, is a different and more dangerous disease: cardiac and skeletal muscle cannot store glycogen, giving exertional syncope, arrhythmia, cardiomyopathy and sudden death after exertion in a young person.

The chain, in one lineGlycogen synthase cannot build the reservoir, so a meal's glucose arrives with nowhere to be stored and overflows into glycolysis to lactate while the blood sugar is still high, and a few hours later there is nothing to draw on — the same defect producing postprandial hyperglycemia and fasting ketotic hypoglycemia within a single day, over a liver of normal size.

Against the look-alikesEvery other glycogenosis presents with an enlarged liver or a loaded muscle; this one has an empty one, so the absence of hepatomegaly is its signature, along with the hyperglycemia-then-hypoglycemia swing across a single day. Against von Gierke, which is also hypoglycemic with lactic acidosis: there the lactate is high while fasting and the hypoglycemia is non-ketotic, whereas here the lactate is high after eating and the hypoglycemia is ketotic.

10
Andersen, GSD IV
Protein lost
branching enzyme
Pathway
Glycogen synthesiz

Branching enzyme lifts a block of roughly seven glucose residues off a growing chain and reattaches it as a new α-1,6 branch. Without it, the chain grows long, straight and unbranched, chemically resembling plant amylopectin. The problem this creates is not energy but solubility: highly branched glycogen is soluble because its many free ends stay hydrated, while a long unbranched glucan precipitates. The insoluble material accumulates inside hepatocytes and behaves like a foreign body, provoking inflammation and fibrosis, so the child develops hepatosplenomegaly, then cirrhosis, portal hypertension and liver failure, with death by around five years unless transplanted. Blood glucose stays largely normal, because the modest glycogen that is present can still be mobilized and gluconeogenesis is untouched.

The chain, in one lineWithout branching enzyme the chain grows long and straight, and an unbranched glucan is insoluble — so it precipitates inside the hepatocyte and is handled as a foreign body, giving inflammation, fibrosis and cirrhosis, while blood glucose stays normal because the failure is structural rather than energetic.

Against the look-alikesThis is the only glycogenosis whose lethal outcome is structural liver disease rather than hypoglycemia or exercise failure — so cirrhosis in a GSD means Andersen. Cori III is its exact opposite in shape: too many short outer chains from failing to remove branches, rather than none at all from failing to make them. The way to hold them apart is that Andersen cannot build a branch and Cori cannot cut one. Against von Gierke, where glycogen is structurally normal and merely excessive.

11
Cori (Forbes), GSD III
Protein lost
debranching enzyme
Pathway
Glycogenolysis

Debranching enzyme carries two activities on one polypeptide: a 4-α-glucanotransferase that shifts three residues across to a neighboring chain, and an α-1,6-glucosidase that clips the exposed branch point off. Phosphorylase can strip a chain only to within four residues of a branch, so without debranching, glycogenolysis halts there and a limit dextrin with SHORT outer chains accumulates. Crucially, some glucose still comes out — everything distal to that fourth residue — and gluconeogenesis is completely intact, so the hypoglycemia is real but far milder than von Gierke, and it is KETOTIC, with normal lactate and normal uric acid. Clinically: hepatomegaly, growth retardation and raised transaminases through childhood, usually improving after puberty as the liver's relative demand falls. Type IIIa involves muscle as well, adding myopathy, cardiomyopathy and a raised CK. A high-protein diet works well here precisely because the gluconeogenic machinery is available to convert those amino acids into glucose, alongside frequent high-carbohydrate meals with cornstarch. Autosomal recessive, about 1 in 100,000 in general but roughly 1 in 5,400 among North African Jews, where about 1 in 35 carries it.

The chain, in one linePhosphorylase can only strip a chain to within four residues of a branch, so without debranching enzyme it halts there and a limit dextrin with short outer chains accumulates — but everything outside that fourth residue is still released and gluconeogenesis is entirely intact, so the hypoglycemia is mild and ketotic with normal lactate and normal urate.

Against the look-alikesVon Gierke shares the big liver and the hypoglycemia, but has lactic acidosis, hyperuricemia, hyperlipidemia and no ketones, and no dietary protein can rescue it because the exit door itself is gone. Hers VI is even milder and confined to the liver, with no myopathy and no raised CK. Andersen IV involves the same branch points but on the way in rather than the way out, and kills through cirrhosis instead of hypoglycemia.

12
McArdle, GSD V
Protein lost
myophosphorylase · PYGM
Pathway
Glycogenolysis, muscle

Muscle glycogen phosphorylase cleaves α-1,4 bonds by phosphorolysis, releasing glucose 1-phosphate already phosphorylated. Losing it means skeletal muscle cannot mobilize its own glycogen at all. In the first minutes of exercise, exactly when muscle should be running on that glycogen, ATP fails and the patient gets cramps, pain and stiffness; pushed further, rhabdomyolysis with myoglobinuria and a risk of acute kidney injury, with CK raised even at rest. The identifying finding is that ischemic forearm exercise produces NO rise in blood lactate, with an exaggerated ammonia rise as the purine nucleotide cycle runs in its place. Blood glucose and cognition are entirely normal, because liver phosphorylase is a separate gene. And after roughly ten minutes of continued mild activity the patient experiences a SECOND WIND, as fatty acid delivery and hepatic glucose output ramp up and take over the work that glycogen was supposed to do. Muscle glycogen is high and structurally normal — it simply cannot be reached. Autosomal recessive, about 1 in 100,000, in childhood-onset and adult-onset forms, and on the whole a benign condition with normal mental development and a normal lifespan, provided rhabdomyolysis is avoided.

The chain, in one lineMuscle cannot mobilize its own glycogen, so in the first minutes of exertion — exactly when glycogen should be carrying the load — ATP fails and the fiber cramps and lyses, with no rise in lactate and a compensatory ammonia rise from the purine nucleotide cycle; then fatty acid delivery and hepatic glucose output ramp up and take over, which is the second wind.

Against the look-alikesTarui produces a nearly identical muscle syndrome with the same flat lactate curve, and the discriminator is a glucose load: it gives McArdle a second wind and makes Tarui worse, because in Tarui the muscle cannot use the glucose either and the insulin response strips away the fat and ketones it was actually surviving on. Tarui also has hemolytic anemia, which McArdle never does. Against Pompe: that is fixed weakness at rest with a huge heart, not cramps on exertion. Against Hers VI, the same enzyme's liver isoform: hepatomegaly and hypoglycemia with normal muscle — one gene each, separated by tissue alone.

13
Hers, GSD VI
(and GSD IX)
Protein lost
liver phosphorylase · PYGL
Pathway
Glycogenolysis, liver

The hepatic counterpart of McArdle. Liver glycogen phosphorylase cannot break down stored glycogen, so glycogen accumulates and the liver enlarges — often strikingly — while gluconeogenesis stays fully available to defend blood glucose. The hypoglycemia is therefore mild and KETOTIC, with normal lactate and normal urate, and the typical presentation is a well-looking toddler with a protuberant abdomen, marked hepatomegaly and some growth delay, improving steadily with age. The deficiency is characteristically partial rather than complete; a complete loss would not be survivable. GSD IX, deficiency of phosphorylase kinase — the enzyme that activates phosphorylase — gives an indistinguishable picture, is the commonest cause in this group, and is most often X-linked.

The chain, in one lineLiver phosphorylase is gone, so glycogen accumulates and the liver enlarges — but gluconeogenesis is untouched and still defends the blood sugar, so the hypoglycemia is mild and ketotic with normal lactate and urate, and the child improves with age as the liver's relative share of glucose demand falls.

Against the look-alikesVon Gierke has a liver just as large but is a severe disease: lactic acidosis, hyperuricemia, hyperlipidemia and non-ketotic hypoglycemia within hours of a feed. Ketones present and lactate normal is what puts you here instead. Cori III sits between them and adds myopathy with a raised CK in type IIIa. And McArdle is the identical enzymatic lesion in the other tissue, which is why it produces exercise cramps with a normal liver and normal glucose.

14
Pompe, GSD II
Protein lost
lysosomal acid α-1,4-glucosidase
Pathway
Lysosomal glycogen turnover

The odd one out, because the missing enzyme is not part of cytoplasmic glycogen metabolizm at all. Acid α-glucosidase, or acid maltase, is a lysosomal hydrolase that digests the glycogen routinely swept into lysosomes by autophagy. Cytoplasmic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis are untouched, which is why blood glucose is NORMAL and glycogen structure is NORMAL — the glycogen is simply trapped in the wrong compartment. The lysosomes swell, distort the contractile apparatus and eventually rupture, and the cells that suffer are the ones turning over the most glycogen: cardiac muscle, skeletal muscle, smooth muscle and, in infancy, motor neurons. The infantile form gives massive CARDIOMEGALY with a hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, a floppy hypotonic baby, macroglossia, hepatomegaly, and death within about two years from cardiorespiratory failure if untreated. Later-onset forms present as a limb-girdle myopathy with disproportionately early diaphragmatic weakness. Enzyme replacement with alglucosidase alfa is the treatment.

The chain, in one lineThe lysosomal enzyme is missing while cytoplasmic glycogen metabolism runs perfectly, so glucose and glycogen structure are both normal and the glycogen simply accumulates in the wrong compartment — swelling and rupturing lysosomes in the tissues that turn over the most, so what fails is the heart and the diaphragm rather than the blood sugar.

Against the look-alikesCardiomegaly alongside a normal blood glucose separates it from every other glycogenosis in one line — the mnemonic that Pompe trashes the pump is doing real work. It is also the only lysosomal storage disease in the group, which is why it behaves like Tay–Sachs or Gaucher rather than like von Gierke. Against McArdle and Tarui: those are exertional cramps in a muscle that is otherwise normal, whereas Pompe is fixed weakness in a muscle being physically destroyed.

VI

Ethanol and methanol

2 diseases
15
Alcoholic hypoglycemia
Protein lost
none — a raised NADH:NAD⁺ ratio
Pathway
Ethanol oxidation

No enzyme is missing here; the lesion is a redox flood. Alcohol dehydrogenase converts ethanol to acetaldehyde and aldehyde dehydrogenase converts acetaldehyde to acetate, and each step reduces NAD⁺, so the cytosolic NADH:NAD⁺ ratio rises steeply. Every reaction that needs NAD⁺ is then pushed backwards, and the consequence that matters is that all three gluconeogenic entry points are stripped simultaneously: pyruvate is driven to lactate, oxaloacetate is driven to malate, and glycerol 3-phosphate can no longer be oxidized back to DHAP. With no pyruvate, no oxaloacetate and no glycerol, gluconeogenesis stops — and in a drinker who has been fasting long enough to have spent his glycogen, blood glucose falls. The same excess NADH produces the other two findings: lactic acidosis, which also blocks renal urate excretion and precipitates gout, and diversion of DHAP into glycerol 3-phosphate for triglyceride assembly, giving a fatty liver. So the identifying picture is hypoglycemia, lactic acidosis and hepatic steatosis together in a fasting drinker. Two related bedside facts follow from the same chemistry: AST:ALT runs above 2:1 because alcohol depletes pyridoxal phosphate and ALT depends on it more heavily; and in alcoholic ketoacidosis the high NADH pushes acetoacetate to β-hydroxybutyrate, which the nitroprusside urine ketone test cannot detect, so the ketone test reads falsely low in a deeply ketotic patient.

The chain, in one lineTwo dehydrogenase steps dump NADH into the cytosol, so every near-equilibrium reaction is pushed toward its reduced product — pyruvate to lactate, oxaloacetate to malate, DHAP to glycerol 3-phosphate — and since those three are precisely the entry points of gluconeogenesis, all of them are stripped at once, so a drinker who has fasted long enough to spend his glycogen has no route left to blood glucose.

Against the look-alikesVon Gierke and pyruvate carboxylase deficiency also give hypoglycemia with lactic acidosis, but those are congenital and absolute, whereas here every enzyme is intact and only the redox balance has moved — so it resolves as the ethanol clears. The requirement for fasting is the tell: a well-fed drinker with full glycogen stores does not become hypoglycemic, because glycogenolysis needs no NAD⁺.

16
Methanol poisoning
Protein lost
none — ADH and ALDH act on it
Pathway
Ethanol oxidation

Methanol itself is comparatively inert; the toxicity lies wholly in what the body makes of it, using the same two enzymes that handle ethanol. Alcohol dehydrogenase converts methanol to formaldehyde and aldehyde dehydrogenase converts formaldehyde to FORMATE, each step generating NADH along the way. Formate is the poison: it inhibits cytochrome c oxidase, producing a severe high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis on top of the lactate that the blocked respiratory chain generates, and it concentrates in the optic nerve. Hence the characteristic course — a latent period of 12 to 24 hours while metabolite accumulates, then visual loss, headache, vomiting and a profound acidosis. Treatment attacks the enzyme rather than the poison: fomepizole, or ethanol if fomepizole is unavailable, competes for alcohol dehydrogenase so that methanol is excreted unchanged, alongside bicarbonate for the acidosis, folate to accelerate formate clearance, and dialysis in severe cases.

The chain, in one lineMethanol is nearly inert until alcohol and aldehyde dehydrogenase convert it to formate, which is why nothing happens for twelve to twenty-four hours; formate then inhibits cytochrome c oxidase, giving a profound anion-gap acidosis, and concentrates in the optic nerve — so the treatment is to block the enzyme rather than remove the poison.

Against the look-alikesEthylene glycol travels through the identical enzyme and receives the identical treatment, but ends as oxalate, so it produces renal failure with calcium oxalate crystalluria rather than blindness — the target organ is what separates them. Against alcoholic hypoglycemia: both raise NADH through the same two enzymes, but there the injury is a downstream metabolic consequence of redox, while here it is a specific toxic product, which is precisely why blocking the enzyme is curative in one and irrelevant in the other.

VII

The sugars that are not glucose

6 diseases
17
Essential fructosuria
Protein lost
fructokinase
Pathway
Fructose entry

Fructokinase phosphorylates fructose to fructose 1-phosphate in liver, kidney and intestine, and phosphorylation is what traps a sugar inside a cell. Without it, fructose is simply never trapped: it stays in the blood, is filtered, and appears in the urine. Nothing accumulates in a form capable of doing harm, no inorganic phosphate is sequestered, and ATP is entirely untouched. The condition is BENIGN and is nearly always discovered by accident — a positive urine test for reducing substances with a negative glucose-oxidase dipstick, since the dipstick reads only glucose and fructose is a reducing sugar. No treatment, no restriction, no consequences.

The chain, in one lineWithout fructokinase, fructose is never phosphorylated, so it is never trapped inside a cell — nothing accumulates, no phosphate is sequestered, no ATP is lost — and the sugar simply leaves in the urine.

Against the look-alikesHereditary fructose intolerance is the very next enzyme in the same pathway and is life-threatening, and the entire difference is whether a trapped phosphorylated intermediate accumulates. The same relationship holds exactly one pathway over, between galactokinase deficiency and classical galactosemia. Learn the pair rather than the four diseases: blocking the kinase is harmless because nothing gets trapped, while blocking the step after the kinase is dangerous because the trapped ester poisons the cell.

18
Hereditary fructose intolerance
Protein lost
aldolase B
Pathway
Fructose entry

Aldolase B splits fructose 1-phosphate into DHAP and glyceraldehyde. The enzyme immediately upstream, fructokinase, is fast and entirely unregulated, so when aldolase B is missing, F1P is manufactured at speed and cannot be cleared, and two things follow. First, every molecule made consumes an inorganic phosphate and locks it away, so intracellular phosphate falls, ATP cannot be regenerated, and the AMP that accumulates is degraded to uric acid — hence hyperuricemia. Second, the phosphate depletion and low ATP inhibit both glycogen phosphorylase and gluconeogenesis, so the liver loses both of its routes to blood glucose and the child becomes profoundly hypoglycemic within about thirty minutes of a fructose load, unresponsive to glucagon. The presentation is therefore an infant who was entirely well on breast milk and begins vomiting, hypoglycemia and jaundice at weaning, when fruit, juice or sucrose is first introduced, with hepatomegaly, coagulopathy, proximal renal tubular dysfunction, lactic acidosis, and liver failure if exposure continues. Children who survive develop a strong aversion to sweet foods and are noted to have remarkably good teeth. Treatment is complete removal of fructose, sucrose and sorbitol.

The chain, in one lineFructokinase upstream is fast and unregulated, so with aldolase B missing, fructose 1-phosphate is manufactured at speed and cannot be cleared; every molecule made locks up an inorganic phosphate, so ATP cannot be regenerated and the AMP that piles up is degraded to urate, while the same phosphate depletion inhibits both glycogen phosphorylase and gluconeogenesis — closing both of the liver's routes to blood glucose within half an hour of a fructose load.

Against the look-alikesEssential fructosuria, one enzyme earlier, does nothing at all — the contrast is the whole lesson. Classical galactosemia is the identical mechanism with a different sugar: a trapped phosphorylated ester, sequestered phosphate, liver and kidney injury. Against von Gierke, which also gives hypoglycemia unresponsive to glucagon: this one is provoked by a specific sugar and disappears entirely when that sugar is removed, which no glycogenosis does.

19
Galactokinase deficiency
Protein lost
galactokinase
Pathway
Galactose, Leloir pathway

Galactokinase makes galactose 1-phosphate. Without it, galactose accumulates unphosphorylated and the only route left open is aldose reductase, which reduces it to GALACTITOL while spending NADPH. Galactitol cannot cross the lens membrane and cannot be metabolized any further, so it accumulates osmotically, draws water into the lens fibers, and they swell and opacify — producing infantile CATARACTS and essentially nothing else. The NADPH consumed may add a small oxidative component in the lens. The child presents with failure to fix and follow, or with an absent red reflex on the newborn examination.

The chain, in one lineGalactose is never phosphorylated, so galactose 1-phosphate never forms and nothing is trapped inside any cell; the surplus is instead reduced by aldose reductase to galactitol, which no enzyme can take further and which swells the lens osmotically — so the disease is cataracts and nothing else.

Against the look-alikesClassical galactosemia produces cataracts by exactly the same galactitol mechanism, but adds liver disease, kidney disease and intellectual disability — because there Gal-1-P does form and accumulate, and it is Gal-1-P, not galactose and not galactitol, that is cytotoxic. So cataracts alone means the kinase; cataracts plus organ damage means the transferase. This is the fructokinase-versus-aldolase-B logic repeated exactly. And the third carbohydrate cause of childhood cataract is diabetes, through the same aldose reductase acting on glucose instead.

20
Classical galactosemia
Protein lost
GALT · galactose-1-P uridyltransferase
Pathway
Galactose, Leloir pathway

GALT performs the middle step of the Leloir pathway, swapping UDP between sugars to convert galactose 1-phosphate plus UDP-glucose into glucose 1-phosphate plus UDP-galactose. Galactokinase upstream continues working normally, so Gal-1-P is produced and then dammed, sequestering both inorganic phosphate and the cell's UDP pool and poisoning hepatocytes and renal tubular cells directly. Surplus galactose meanwhile spills into aldose reductase and becomes galactitol, giving cataracts by the same osmotic route as galactokinase deficiency. The presentation is a neonate who begins breast milk or lactose-containing formula and develops vomiting, poor feeding, failure to thrive, jaundice and hepatomegaly progressing towards cirrhosis, with hypoglycemia, a proximal renal tubular acidosis, cataracts and, untreated, intellectual disability. Neonatal E. coli sepsis is characteristic, because Gal-1-P impairs neutrophil function. It is on the newborn screen. Treatment is lifelong exclusion of galactose and lactose — but premature ovarian failure and speech and learning difficulties still occur, because the body synthesizes its own Gal-1-P from UDP-glucose regardless of what is eaten.

The chain, in one lineGalactokinase keeps working, so galactose 1-phosphate is made at full rate and then dammed behind the missing transferase; it sequesters phosphate and UDP and injures hepatocytes and renal tubular cells directly, while the overflow galactose still becomes galactitol and clouds the lens — the same sugar producing organ failure here and cataracts alone one enzyme earlier, purely because the trapped ester now exists.

Against the look-alikesGalactokinase deficiency gives the cataracts alone and no systemic disease, for the reason given above. Hereditary fructose intolerance is its structural twin one pathway over and shares the trapped-phosphate mechanism almost step for step. Against lactose intolerance, which involves the same disaccharide but never leaves the lumen: one is a gut symptom, the other is organ failure, and the difference is only whether the sugar was absorbed.

21
Diabetic cataract, retinopathy and neuropathy
Protein lost
none — aldose reductase, unopposed
Pathway
Polyol (sorbitol) pathway

Nothing is missing here either; an enzyme is simply running in a tissue that lacks its partner. Aldose reductase reduces glucose to sorbitol, spending NADPH, and sorbitol dehydrogenase then oxidizes sorbitol to fructose, making NADH. Aldose reductase has a high Km, so at normal blood glucose almost nothing travels this road — but in sustained hyperglycemia the flux rises sharply. In the tissues that have little or no sorbitol dehydrogenase — lens, retina, Schwann cells of peripheral nerve, and kidney — sorbitol is produced and then trapped, because it is polar and cannot diffuse back out. It accumulates osmotically, drawing water in and swelling the cell, and at the same time the NADPH it consumes is drawn from the very pool that glutathione reduction depends on, so the tissue is left less able to handle oxidative stress just as it is being osmotically stressed. That is the mechanism behind diabetic cataract, retinopathy, peripheral neuropathy and part of the nephropathy. Note also that all four of these tissues take up glucose independently of insulin, so their intracellular glucose tracks the blood level directly with nothing to buffer it.

The chain, in one lineAldose reductase has a high Km and no allosteric brake, so sustained hyperglycemia drives flux hard in exactly those tissues that lack sorbitol dehydrogenase; the sorbitol they make can be neither oxidized onward nor diffused out, so it accumulates osmotically while the NADPH spent making it is drawn from the pool glutathione needs — so osmotic and oxidative injury arrive together, in tissues that take up glucose without insulin and therefore cannot buffer the load.

Against the look-alikesGalactosemia and galactokinase deficiency use this same enzyme on a different substrate, producing galactitol instead of sorbitol — which is why a cataract in a child has three carbohydrate causes: galactokinase deficiency, classical galactosemia and diabetes. Against every genetic enzymopathy on this chart: nothing here is broken, so lowering the blood glucose reverses the flux, and glycemic control is the treatment.

22
High dietary fructose
(gout, obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension)
Protein lost
none — fructokinase is unregulated
Pathway
Fructose entry, below PFK-1

No enzyme is missing; the disease is the dose. Sucrose supplies about a tenth of the Western diet and high-fructose corn syrup, blended at 55:45 fructose to glucose, supplies more — a single 12 oz can of soda carries roughly 20 g of fructose, and a can a day is about 55,000 calories a year, some fifteen pounds of fat. All of it goes essentially to the liver, and the damage follows from two facts about the pathway there: fructokinase is fast and entirely unregulated, and its product enters glycolysis below PFK-1, past the only real brake in carbohydrate metabolism. So the flux is set by intake alone. Four consequences follow. Fructokinase consumes ATP and inorganic phosphate faster than the hepatocyte replaces them, so ATP falls through ADP to AMP, and AMP deaminase degrades the surplus to uric acid — hyperuricemia, and urate deposition in joints as gout. That same urate then inhibits endothelial NO synthase, so less nitric oxide is made and less vasodilation follows, which is the link to hypertension and the reason children drinking large volumes of fructose-sweetened soft drinks show higher blood pressures. Meanwhile the carbon runs unbraked to acetyl-CoA, leaves the mitochondrion as citrate and is built into fat, while the glyceraldehyde half is reduced to glycerol and phosphorylated by glycerol kinase to supply the triglyceride backbone — so triglyceride and VLDL rise together, giving obesity and dyslipidemia. And fructose 1-phosphate drives formation of the serine-phosphorylated form of IRS-1, which is the inactive configuration of the insulin signal, so the same sugar produces insulin insensitivity and closes the loop into metabolic syndrome.

The chain, in one lineFructokinase is fast, unregulated, and delivers its carbon below PFK-1, so intake alone sets the flux: ATP and phosphate are consumed faster than they are replaced and the AMP left over becomes urate — which causes gout and, by inhibiting eNOS, hypertension — while the unbraked carbon runs on to citrate and triglyceride for obesity and dyslipidemia, and fructose 1-phosphate inactivates IRS-1 for insulin resistance.

Against the look-alikesHereditary fructose intolerance is the same chemistry made catastrophic by a missing aldolase B: there the phosphate sequestration is acute and profound and the child is hypoglycemic within half an hour, where here it is chronic and low-grade and the blood sugar is normal or high. Von Gierke reaches the same hyperuricemia and hyperlipidemia from the opposite direction — a blocked exit rather than an unbraked entrance. And against a glucose load of the same size: glucose is throttled at PFK-1 and fructose is not, which is the entire reason two sugars of identical calorie content do not behave identically.

VIII

NADPH, and the things it defends

5 diseases
23
G6PD deficiency
Protein lost
glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase
Pathway
Pentose phosphate pathway

G6PD is the rate-limiting committed step of the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway, and clinically its only job that matters is making NADPH. NADPH reduces glutathione; reduced glutathione detoxifies hydrogen peroxide. The red cell is uniquely exposed on all three counts: it carries oxygen for a living, it has no nucleus and so cannot replace the enzyme as the original protein degrades across its 120 days, and it has no other source of NADPH at all. When an oxidative load arrives — infection above all, plus fava beans, primaquine, dapsone, sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin, methylene blue, or diabetic ketoacidosis — glutathione cannot be regenerated, hemoglobin oxidizes and precipitates as HEINZ bodies, and splenic macrophages bite those inclusions out, leaving bite cells on the smear. The clinical event is jaundice with dark brown urine roughly a day after the trigger, self-limiting over about a week as the oldest cells finish lysing. X-linked, so overwhelmingly males, and the commonest disease-producing enzyme defect in the world at some 400 million people, retained because it protects against falciparum malaria. The testing trap is essential: do not assay during the episode — the most deficient cells have already lysed and the reticulocytes replacing them are enzyme-rich, so the result reads falsely normal. Wait several weeks.

The chain, in one lineG6PD supplies the red cell's only NADPH and NADPH is what regenerates glutathione, so an oxidative load leaves peroxide uncleared; hemoglobin oxidizes and precipitates as Heinz bodies, splenic macrophages bite them out, and the episode ends itself as the oldest and most deficient cells finish lysing — which is also exactly why an assay drawn during the crisis reads falsely normal.

Against the look-alikesPyruvate kinase deficiency is chronic, trigger-independent and shows no inclusions, is autosomal recessive, and has a high 2,3-BPG; G6PD is episodic, trigger-dependent, X-linked, with Heinz bodies and bite cells. Sickle cell disease is the comparison worth doing in full, because both are inherited red-cell instabilities that present in crisis and the mechanisms have nothing in common. The lesion there is structural — a point mutation in the β chain of hemoglobin, glutamate to valine at position 6, giving HbS — where here it is metabolic, a failure of NADPH supply. The trigger differs accordingly: HbS polymerizes when it is deoxygenated, so crises follow hypoxia, high altitude, heavy exertion and infection, while G6PD hemolyses under an oxidative load. So does the mechanism of destruction — exposed valine residues let deoxygenated HbS chains clump into tactoids and distort the cell into a sickle, which occludes vessels, against oxidized hemoglobin precipitating as inclusions that the spleen bites out. That difference is visible on the smear, which is exactly what a question will turn on: sickled cells and target cells in one, Heinz bodies and bite cells in the other. Clinically sickle cell brings severe pain crises, splenic sequestration, infection, pulmonary hypertension, stroke and renal dysfunction on top of the anemia; G6PD brings jaundice, dark urine and a self-limited hemolysis. Genetics: sickle cell is autosomal recessive and common in African Americans at about 1 in 500 births, G6PD is X-linked with over 400 known variants and is the commonest disease-producing enzyme defect in the world. Treatment: hydroxyurea to raise HbF and transfusion when needed, versus phototherapy for neonatal hyperbilirubinemia and, above all, avoidance. Prevention is the neatest contrast of the pair — keep the sickle cell patient out of hypoxia, and keep the G6PD patient away from oxidants. Two footnotes worth carrying: the severe Mediterranean variant can hemolyse chronically without any trigger at all, and methylene blue — the standard antidote for methemoglobinemia — is dangerous in G6PD deficiency, because it needs NADPH to work.

24
Thiamine deficiency
Protein lost
TPP — cofactor for four enzymes
Pathway
PDH · TCA · PPP

Thiamine pyrophosphate is a cofactor rather than an enzyme, and it serves four enzymes at once: PDH E1, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase E1, branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase, and transketolase in the pentose phosphate pathway. Losing it therefore shuts the door from glycolysis into the TCA cycle and then stalls the cycle itself one step in, so pyruvate and lactate rise and oxidative ATP production collapses in the tissues with the highest and least interruptible oxidative demand — brain and heart. Dry beriberi is the resulting symmetric peripheral neuropathy; wet beriberi is high-output cardiac failure with edema; Wernicke encephalopathy is the triad of confusion, ophthalmoplegia and ataxia, which if untreated becomes Korsakoff syndrome with confabulation and irreversible anterograde amnesia from mammillary body damage. The assay is elegant and worth remembering because it uses the fourth enzyme rather than the obvious ones: red cell transketolase activity, measured before and after adding TPP. Causes are alcoholism above all, then malnutrition, hyperemesis and bariatric surgery. Give thiamine before glucose in a malnourished or alcoholic patient, because a glucose load consumes what little thiamine remains and can precipitate Wernicke.

The chain, in one lineOne missing cofactor disables PDH, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, the branched-chain complex and transketolase all at once, so the gate into the cycle shuts and the cycle stalls a step in as well — pyruvate and lactate rise and oxidative ATP fails first in brain and heart, which is why a glucose load makes it worse rather than better: it consumes the last of the thiamine.

Against the look-alikesGenetic PDH deficiency produces the same pyruvate block but is congenital, confined to one complex, and presents in the neonate; thiamine deficiency is acquired, reversible, hits four enzymes and adds the transketolase assay. Arsenic poisons three of the same complexes, but by binding lipoic acid rather than by removing TPP, and brings skin, nail and gastrointestinal findings with it. Biotin deficiency is the parallel case one enzyme over, impairing pyruvate carboxylase instead.

25
Chronic granulomatous disease
Protein lost
NADPH oxidase
Pathway
Respiratory burst

NADPH oxidase sits in the phagosome membrane and spends NADPH to reduce molecular oxygen to superoxide — the respiratory burst, and the first step of the chain that yields hydrogen peroxide and then, through myeloperoxidase, hypochlorite. Without it the neutrophil can still find and engulf organisms perfectly well but cannot kill them. The failure is selective in an instructive way: it is worst against catalase-POSITIVE organisms — Staphylococcus aureus, Serratia, Burkholderia cepacia, Nocardia, Aspergillus — because catalase-negative organisms obligingly generate their own hydrogen peroxide, which the crippled phagocyte can still borrow and use, whereas catalase-positive organisms destroy that peroxide and leave the phagocyte with nothing at all. Clinically: severe, recurrent and persistent infections from infancy, with abscesses and granuloma formation, since macrophages wall off what they cannot destroy. The test is the nitroblue tetrazolium reduction assay, which stays YELLOW because no superoxide is made to reduce the dye, or the more sensitive dihydrorhodamine flow cytometry, which shows no fluorescence. Most cases are X-linked. Manage with prophylactic trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, itraconazole and interferon-γ.

The chain, in one lineThe oxidase cannot assemble, so no superoxide is made, so no hydrogen peroxide and no hypochlorite — the phagocyte engulfs normally and then kills nothing; catalase-positive organisms are the ones that matter because catalase-negative organisms supply the peroxide the crippled cell can still borrow, and the organisms that survive inside drive the granulomas.

Against the look-alikesG6PD deficiency starves the identical NADPH supply one pathway earlier, but its clinical face is hemolysis rather than infection — because the red cell has no alternative source of NADPH while the neutrophil's shortfall only becomes visible under maximal burst demand. The two diseases meet at the extreme: severe G6PD deficiency can produce a CGD-like susceptibility to infection, which is the clearest demonstration that these are one supply chain with two failure points.

26
ALS (Lou Gehrig disease)
Protein lost
SOD1 — point mutation
Pathway
ROS clearance

Superoxide dismutase converts superoxide to hydrogen peroxide, the first step in disposing of the radical that mitochondria leak continuously; catalase and glutathione peroxidase then finish the job, the latter using the glutathione that NADPH keeps reduced. About a fifth of familial ALS carries a point mutation in SOD1. The critical point, and the reason this entry breaks the pattern of every other one on this chart, is that the harm does not come from loss of the reaction — measured dismutase activity is often near normal, which is exactly why assaying it does not diagnose the disease — but from a toxic gain of function, in which the misfolded protein aggregates and poisons motor neurons. Clinically: progressive degeneration of both upper and lower motor neurons together, so the patient shows spasticity and hyperreflexia alongside atrophy and fasciculations in the same limb, with sensation preserved and, usually, cognition preserved as well.

The chain, in one lineThe point mutation leaves dismutase activity largely intact, so the injury is not the loss of the reaction but the misfolded protein aggregating — which is why the enzyme assay is normal and cannot diagnose it, and why motor neurons, with the longest axons and the highest metabolic load in the nervous system, are the cells that fail first.

Against the look-alikesIn G6PD deficiency and chronic granulomatous disease the missing activity is the disease; in SOD1 ALS the missing activity is largely beside the point and the misfolded protein is the disease. That single distinction is what the card is really testing, because it is the one place in this material where the mechanism you would predict from the pathway is the wrong answer.

27
Rhabdomyolysis on a statin
Protein lost
none — CYP3A4 inhibited
Pathway
Cytochrome P450

Another entry with no missing protein, running through the same NADPH economy. The cytochrome P450 monooxygenases spend NADPH and molecular oxygen to hydroxylate lipophilic substrates and make them excretable, and CYP3A4 handles a large fraction of prescribed drugs, including most statins. Add an inhibitor — grapefruit juice, an azole antifungal, a macrolide, a protease inhibitor, or certain calcium channel blockers — and the statin is no longer cleared at the expected rate, so plasma levels climb into the toxic range and skeletal muscle is injured. The patient develops myalgia and proximal weakness with a rising CK, and in severe cases frank rhabdomyolysis with myoglobinuria, hyperkalemia and acute kidney injury.

The chain, in one lineBlocking CYP3A4 removes the statin's clearance route, so plasma levels climb into a toxic range and myocytes are injured — CK rises, fibers lyse, and the myoglobin that is filtered obstructs and directly poisons the tubules, so a drug interaction ends as acute kidney injury.

Against the look-alikesMcArdle and Tarui also present with muscle pain, a raised CK and myoglobinuria, but those are lifelong and provoked by exertion, while this appears in an adult after a new prescription or a change in diet. Enzyme induction is the mirror image of the same interaction — rifampin, phenytoin, carbamazepine and St John's wort accelerate CYP3A4 and produce therapeutic failure rather than toxicity, which is the same pharmacology read backwards.
