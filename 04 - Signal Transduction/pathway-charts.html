Objective 2 and 3

Gs and cAMP

Glucagon runs the identical cascade through its own receptor.

Swipe the chart sideways to see all of it.

From the lecture

  • Cholera toxin ADP-ribosylates Gsα on an Arg at the GTPase active site, freezing it in the active GTP-bound state. cAMP rises, PKA phosphorylates the apical chloride channel, and Cl⁻, HCO3⁻, Na⁺ and water pour into the lumen as rice-water stool. Slides 13–14 · Card 41
  • Inactive PKA is R₂C₂. cAMP binds the regulatory subunits and the conformational change dissociates the catalytic subunits, which are then enzymatically active. Activation here is de-repression, not flipping a switch. Slide 21 + notes · Card 24
  • Glucagon comes from the pancreatic α cells in response to low blood glucose. Receptors are in liver and adipose tissue; slide 11 marks them absent in the muscle, because muscle keeps its glycogen for itself. Slides 11, 16 · Card 35
  • β-1 sits on cardiac muscle and β-2 on the smooth muscle of lung and intestine. Both raise cAMP, but the downstream pathways diverge — which is why epinephrine both relieves bronchospasm in asthma and restores cardiac rhythm after arrest. Slide 26 · Card 34
  • Sort the four kinases by what activates them: PKA by cAMP, PKB by PI3 kinase’s lipid product via PDK1, PKC by DAG and phosphatidylserine, PKG by cGMP. The discriminator is always the activator, never the target. Card 73

Objective 2 and 3

Gq, IP₃ and DAG

One cleavage, two messengers, both arms ending in phosphorylation.

Swipe the chart sideways to see all of it.

From the lecture

  • PLC cuts on the phosphate side of the glycerol backbone, so the phosphate leaves with the head group. The inositol head already carries two phosphates; the one taken from the backbone makes three. That is where trisphosphate comes from — it is derived, not memorised. Card 26
  • Cytosolic Ca²⁺ rises from about 0.1 µM to over 1 µM. Calcium phosphorylates nothing itself: it binds calmodulin, and the Ca²⁺-CaM complex activates the kinase. Keep that handoff explicit. Slide 23 + notes · Card 29
  • PKC is activated by the membrane components DAG and phosphatidylserine; some isoforms also require Ca²⁺. Because DAG never leaves the membrane, activation is a recruitment event at the membrane surface. Slide 24 · Card 30
  • Slide 11 pairs the isoforms explicitly: epinephrine at α-1 uses PLC-β, and the bottom line of the same slide reads insulin — phospholipase C-γ. The discriminator is the receptor class upstream, not the product; both make the same IP3 and DAG. Slide 11 · Card 27
  • Termination is step 8 of the α-1 cascade: IP3 is dephosphorylated in sequence to IP2, then IP, then inositol — the same design principle as phosphodiesterase in the cAMP pathway. Card 31

Objective 2 and 3

Gi

Same enzyme as Gs, opposite sign.

Swipe the chart sideways to see all of it.

From the lecture

  • Pertussis toxin ADP-ribosylates Giα on a Cys, freezing it inactive and GDP-bound and inhibiting subunit dissociation. Its inhibition of adenylyl cyclase is lost, so cAMP rises anyway. The net result of both toxins is overproduction of cAMP. Slide 13 · Card 42
  • The α-2 receptor is one of the two ways epinephrine signaling is terminated. The same hormone that switches the cell on through β switches it off through α-2; the tissue’s receptor expression decides which. Card 33
  • Users of this route: epinephrine at α-2, prostaglandins, opiates, and many neurotransmitters including acetylcholine, dopamine and serotonin. Card 20
  • Slide 33 puts four G protein diseases in one figure — cholera, pertussis, oncogenic Ras, neurofibromatosis. Every one is a protein that cannot get back to GDP, or an inhibitor that cannot do its job. Two are trimeric, two monomeric; the switch rule does not care. Slide 33 · Cards 43, 70

Objective 4

RTK, Ras and MAP kinase

Dimerization is the activating event, which is why disease exploits that step.

Swipe the chart sideways to see all of it.

From the lecture

  • RTKs have a single transmembrane span with the binding site on the external surface. The unoccupied receptor is a monomer; ligand binding forms a dimer, and dimerization activates the cytosolic tyrosine kinases by phosphorylation. The phosphotyrosines are then read by SH2 adaptor proteins. Slides 30–32 · Card 45
  • GEF exchanges GDP for GTP to turn a G protein on; GAP drives hydrolysis to turn it off. SOS is the GEF for Ras; neurofibromin behaves as the GAP. Card 16
  • The oncogenic change is at amino acid 12, where GGC (glycine) becomes GTC (valine). The mutant cannot return to the GDP-bound state. Note what is not broken: receptor, Grb2, SOS and Raf are all normal. The lesion is purely the off-switch. Slide 33 · Card 48
  • Achondroplasia: a single substitution in the transmembrane domain of FGFR3 gives ligand-independent activation. There are four FGF receptors and at least 19 FGF ligands; FGFR1–3 give craniosynostosis syndromes, and FGFR3 alone gives achondroplasia. Slide 35 · Cards 50–51

Objective 5

Insulin

Insulin signaling via MAP kinase and PI 3-kinase pathways.

Swipe the chart sideways to see all of it.

From the lecture

  • Slide 40 gives the machinery in full: phosphatase / PI3 kinase and protein kinase B / Ras Raf MAP kinase / phospholipase C-γ. Slide 11 confirms the last one on its bottom line. Slides 11, 40
  • The insulin receptor follows the tyrosine kinase model but is already a dimer before ligand binds — two membrane-spanning α-β pairs, α extracellular, β carrying the kinase. A growth factor receptor is a monomer that must dimerize. That resting state is the clean discriminator. Slide 37 · Cards 52, 77
  • Slide 38 draws the chain: insulin → tyrosine kinase activity → IRS → activated PI 3-kinase → PI-3,4,5-trisP → PH domains recruit PDK1 and PKB → PDK1 activates PKB → dissociation → active PKB. Phosphorylated IRS creates binding sites for Grb2, PLC-γ and PI3 kinase. Slide 38 · Card 53
  • Do not mix these two up: PI3 kinase is an SH2 domain adaptor protein; IP3 is a small soluble second messenger. One phosphorylates a membrane lipid, the other opens the ER calcium channel. Cards 54, 72
  • Both slides’ wording is worth holding: slide 38 labels the endpoint GLUT4 expression, skeletal muscle and adipose tissue; slide 39 is titled GLUT4 recruitment and says PK-B is involved with recruitment of GLUT-4 transporters. Akt is also called protein kinase B. Slides 38–39 · Card 55
  • The slide 39 notes state that the mechanism connecting Akt to cellular trafficking of GLUT4 is not known, and the figure marks two steps with a question mark. Do not invent a step to fill it. Slide 39 + notes · Card 55
  • Exercise recruits a separate pool of GLUT4 without the insulin pathway: contraction raises intracellular Ca²⁺ and activates PKC, and a changed AMP/ATP ratio activates AMPK. This is why exercise lowers glucose in insulin-resistant patients — the broken route is bypassed. Slide 39 notes · Card 56
  • Insulin pathways are active dephosphorylated because insulin activates a phosphatase — the mirror of glucagon and epinephrine, whose pathways are active phosphorylated. Insulin also activates cAMP phosphodiesterase, a second route to lowering cAMP. Knowing which hormone is up tells you the phosphorylation state of everything it touches. Slide 40 · Cards 23, 38, 57
  • Insulin is anabolic to lipid and protein; for carbohydrate it promotes utilisation for energy. Its growth-factor-like activity through the Ras-Raf-MAP kinase arm is why an infant of a diabetic mother may be large. Card 57

Cytokine signaling

JAK-STAT

The receptor borrows a kinase, and the substrate is already a transcription factor.

Swipe the chart sideways to see all of it.

From the lecture

  • The four steps as the slide figure numbers them: 1. receptors bind cytokines, dimerize and bind Jaks. 2. Jaks phosphorylate each other and the receptor. 3. Receptor binds and phosphorylates STATs. 4. STATs dissociate, dimerize and translocate to the nucleus. Slide 41 figure · Card 59
  • The receptors have associated rather than intrinsic kinase activity, forming heterodimers. The kinase is a separate protein recruited to the receptor, not built into it — this is the whole basis of that split in the slide 5 four-class scheme. Slides 5, 41 · Card 58
  • The pathways are much shorter and simpler than those triggered by RTKs, so the response is much more rapid. Compare Grb2 → SOS → Ras → Raf → MAP kinase. STAT is signal carrier and transcription factor in one molecule. Slide 41 · Card 59
  • The names describe the source cell, not the chemistry. Lymphocytes give lymphokines, monocytes and macrophages give monokines, and many lymphokines are also interleukins — growth factors targeted to cells of haematopoietic origin. Slide 41 notes · Card 60

Serine/threonine receptors

TGF-β and Smad

The fourth receptor class in the slide 5 scheme.

Swipe the chart sideways to see all of it.

From the lecture

  • Note the order carefully: the ligand binds type II, type II phosphorylates type I, and type I phosphorylates the R-Smad. Getting these the wrong way round is the usual error. TGF-β itself is two identical subunits. Slide 42 · Card 61
  • R-Smad is the receptor-specific Smad; it complexes with the Co-Smad, Smad 4, and migrates to the nucleus to activate or inhibit target genes. Both directions are possible. Card 61
  • Slide 42 attaches this pathway to osteogenesis and developmental processes. Slide 8 lists Smad among the SH2 domain proteins a membrane receptor can activate, alongside Grb2, STAT, PI3 kinase and phosphatase. Slides 8, 42

Local signaling

NO and cGMP

Viagra and nitroglycerin enter the same chain at opposite ends.

Swipe the chart sideways to see all of it.

From the lecture

  • The chain on slide 44: arginine + O₂ → [NOS, NADPH] → nitric oxide and citrulline → soluble guanylate cyclase → GTP to cGMP → protein kinase G → phosphorylation of proteins → vasodilatation. The cofactor is NADPH, not NADH. Slide 44 · Card 62
  • NO is a freely diffusible gas, so it crosses membranes unaided and binds a soluble guanylyl cyclase in the cytosol rather than a membrane-bound one. Half-life 2 to 30 seconds, which confines it to strictly local signaling. Slide 43 · Card 63
  • PKG does not relax the muscle directly. It phosphorylates smooth muscle proteins and activates a phosphatase, and that phosphatase is what inhibits actin-myosin interaction. The vessels then expand — endothelium-derived relaxing factor. Slide 43 · Card 64
  • Nitroglycerin relieves angina by producing NO and acts as a venodilator; isosorbide mononitrate dilates vessels to reduce blood pressure. Sildenafil, vardenafil and tadalafil inhibit PDE5, raising cGMP by slowing inactivation, not increasing synthesis — which is why combining them with nitrates stacks the effect. Slide 43 · Cards 66–67, 82
  • Slide 44’s second figure gives the five sites of termination: release, messenger (diffusion, degradation), receptor (desensitization, downregulation), signal transduction (protein phosphatases, GTPases) and second messenger (phosphodiesterases). Slide 44 figure · Card 68

Objective 1

Intracellular receptors

Solubility decides where the receptor is and whether the signal reaches the genome.

Swipe the chart sideways to see all of it.

From the lecture

  • Four properties of a lipophilic signal: diffuses through the bilayer to an intracellular receptor, requires a transport protein, has a longer half life, and acts at the nucleus. The long half-life follows from the carrier — bound hormone is protected from degradation. Slide 6 · Card 7
  • The steroid-thyroid superfamily: cortisol (glucocorticoid), aldosterone (mineralocorticoid), T3, vitamin D3, and the retinoids — all-trans and 9-cis retinoic acid. Slide 6 · Card 7
  • Hydrophilic hormones bind plasma membrane receptors, need no transport protein, and have a shorter half life. They cannot act on the nucleus directly — but they still change gene expression, by relay through CREB. Slide 7 · Cards 8, 25
  • Chronic stress releases cortisol, using an intracellular receptor and modulation of gene expression — the only row on slide 40 that does not use a plasma membrane receptor, and the only one whose effect is slow. Slide 40 · Card 39
  • Only the cells that exhibit the appropriate receptor respond. A hormone circulates to every cell; that it acts on some and not others is a fact about receptor expression, not delivery. Slide 5 · Card 6
